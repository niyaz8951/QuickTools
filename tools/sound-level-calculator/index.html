Background

Enough theory to read a data sheet correctly and defend a number in a review. Sources are named at the end of each panel.

Sound power, sound pressure and sound intensity

Three quantities describe the same phenomenon and are constantly confused for one another, because all three are expressed in decibels.

Sound power, Lw is the acoustic energy the source emits. It belongs to the machine and does not change wherever you install it. Reference 10−12 W. This is what equipment catalogues publish.

Sound pressure, Lp is what an ear or a microphone registers at one particular spot. It depends on distance, on room finish, and on everything the sound had to pass through. Reference 20 µPa, which is 2 × 10−5 Pa — roughly the quietest sound a healthy young ear can detect at 1 kHz.

Sound intensity, Li is the flow of acoustic energy through an area, and unlike the other two it has a direction. Reference 10−12 W/m². Mostly used for measurement rather than specification.

The decibel itself is just a ratio on a log scale:

dB = 10 · log10 (measured ÷ reference)

Every criterion you will ever be handed — NC, RC, dB(A), a boundary limit — is written in sound pressure. Every catalogue you will ever open is written in sound power. Bridging those two is the entire job of this tool, and catalogue figures look alarming next to an NC 30 specification only because they are not the same quantity.

Reference: ISO 3741 and ISO 3744 for power determination; ANSI/ASA S1.1 for terminology. Note that a small number of published training decks state the sound pressure reference as 10−12 Pa. That is a typographical slip for the power reference — the pressure reference is 20 µPa.

Octave bands and weighting

Fan noise is spread across the frequency range, not concentrated in one tone, and both the ear and the criteria curves treat each part of that range differently. Splitting the sound into octave bands — each one double the frequency of the last — lets you see the shape.

Shape matters because attenuation is frequency-dependent. A lined duct swallows high frequencies easily and does almost nothing at 63 Hz. Two units with identical overall dB(A) can behave completely differently on site.

A-weighting discounts the low frequencies the ear is less sensitive to, so that one number tracks perceived loudness reasonably well. It descends from the equal-loudness contours published by Fletcher and Munson in 1933. C-weighting is much flatter and is used where low frequency content matters.

NC and RC are defined on unweighted band levels. If your data sheet publishes A-weighted bands and you rate them directly, the answer will be wrong and too optimistic. Set the weighting selector above to match your sheet and the correction is removed for you.

Quick check: log-sum the bands as published. If they add up to the sheet's own overall dB(A) figure, the bands are already A-weighted. If the sum lands several dB higher, they are unweighted.

Reference: ANSI S1.11 and IEC 61260 for band definitions; IEC 61672-1 for the weighting curves.

Adding levels together

Decibels are logarithmic, so they do not add arithmetically. Two identical sources give 3 dB more than one, not double.

Ltotal = 10 · log10 ( 10L₁/10 + 10L₂/10 + … )

For mental arithmetic, work from the difference between the two levels and add to the larger one: equal or within 1 dB, add 3; 2 to 4 dB apart, add 2; 5 to 9 dB apart, add 1; 10 dB or more apart, add nothing that matters.

That last rule is the useful one in review. If your carefully attenuated supply path arrives 13 dB below the total, it is contributing nothing, and every hour spent improving it is wasted until the dominant path is dealt with.

Tones are a separate problem from level. A pure tone becomes perceptible when it stands roughly 5 to 6 dB above the neighbouring critical bands, and many people find any perceptible tone objectionable regardless of how quiet it is. The ear behaves as a bank of band-pass filters, and at mid frequencies each filter is close to a third-octave wide — so a tone that sits inside one octave band can be masked in the published data and still be plainly audible on site.

NC — how the rating works

Noise criteria curves were developed by Leo Beranek at Bolt Beranek and Newman in 1957, in response to HVAC noise complaints in post-war office buildings. Each curve is a set of maximum octave band sound pressure levels from 63 Hz to 8 kHz. The curves slope downward with frequency, reflecting that the ear tolerates low frequency rumble more readily than high frequency hiss. The designating number was set to correspond roughly to the speech interference level.

The rating method is tangency. Plot your spectrum, and your NC rating is the lowest curve your spectrum fits entirely underneath. A single band poking over the line sets the rating for the whole spectrum, which is why this tool always reports which band is governing. Good practice is to quote both, as NC 45 (125 Hz), because the band tells the next person where to look.

NC's weakness is that it treats every spectrum below a given curve as equivalent. One can be rumbly and one can be hissy and both rate NC 30. That is what RC was invented to fix.

Reference: ANSI/ASA S12.2, Criteria for Evaluating Room Noise, which is the governing document today and sets out an A-weighted survey method, an engineering method using the NC curves, and an RNC method for low-frequency fluctuating noise.

RC Mark II — the number and the letter

Room criteria were introduced by Warren Blazier for ASHRAE in 1981 and refined as RC Mark II in 1997. Unlike NC, the number is not a tangency. It is simply the arithmetic mean of the 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz bands.

The letter is where the value is. A reference contour is drawn as a straight line through that level at 1 kHz, falling 5 dB per octave, with the 16 Hz point held level with 31.5 Hz rather than extrapolated. The spectrum is compared against that contour in three regions:

  • LF — 16, 31.5 and 63 Hz. Excess here reads as rumble.
  • MF — 125, 250 and 500 Hz. Excess here reads as roar.
  • HF — 1000, 2000 and 4000 Hz. Excess here reads as hiss.

Each region gets a spectral deviation factor, which is the energy average of the differences across its three bands. The quality assessment index is the range between the highest and lowest of those three factors. If the index is 5 dB or less the spectrum is balanced and rates (N) for neutral. Above 5, the region with the largest positive factor names the character. An index up to 10 dB is marginal; beyond 10 dB, ASHRAE expects an average occupant to find it objectionable.

This matters more than the number on hotel and residential work. A rumbly RC 30 draws complaints that a neutral RC 33 does not. The tool computes the full RC Mark II rating including the index, and tells you when it is doing so without the two lowest bands.

Reference: ASHRAE Handbook — Applications, Chapter 49, Noise and Vibration Control, Tables 2 and 3. The worked example in the example loader reproduces the handbook's own published case, which returns RC 35(LF) with an index of 7.2.

The room equation

Sound power entering a room builds up until the energy leaving through the surfaces and absorbed inside it balances the energy going in. The result is the room effect:

Lp = Lw + 10 · log10 ( Q / 4πr² + 4 / R )

The first term is the direct field — what reaches you straight from the source. Q is directivity: 1 in free space, 2 flush in a surface, 4 at the junction of two, 8 in a corner. r is the distance to the listener.

The second term is the reverberant field, set by the room constant:

R = S̄α / (1 − ̄α)

where S is total surface area and ̄α is the average absorption coefficient. A furnished hotel guest room at 1.5 m from a ceiling grille typically gives about −6.5 dB. That is the entire room effect, and it is much smaller than most people assume — it is not the 15 or 20 dB that would rescue a bad selection.

Outdoors there is no reverberant term at all. Level falls 6 dB every time the distance doubles, minus air absorption and any barrier.

Fan laws, duct velocity and data quality

Fan sound power tracks airflow and static pressure:

Lw ∝ 10 · log10(flow) + 20 · log10(static pressure)

Airflow is set by the load and you cannot argue with it. Static pressure is set by your system design, and it carries twice the weight. Every unnecessary pressure drop you design out is bought back as quiet, which is why coil face velocity, filter selection and duct sizing are acoustic decisions as much as energy ones.

Duct velocity is the other half of that. Push air too fast and the ductwork generates the noise you installed attenuators to remove. The table in the result section above shows the ASHRAE guidance for whichever criterion you have selected.

How much precision does the data deserve? AMCA 303 is blunt about it: differences in sound power of 2 dB or less are not significant; when comparing between manufacturers, disregard differences under 4 dB; and in the 63 Hz band, disregard differences of 6 dB or less. AMCA also notes that a test room without pure tone qualification can carry ±8 dB of uncertainty in the third-octave band containing the blade pass frequency.

So a bid comparison that turns on a 3 dB difference between two manufacturers is not a real difference. Say so in the review rather than ranking on noise.

Reference: AMCA 303, Applications of Sound Power Level Ratings. Test methods: AMCA 300 and AHRI 260 for ducted air-moving equipment, AHRI 370 for large outdoor equipment, ISO 3741 for reverberant rooms, ISO 9614 for intensity.

What this tool does not calculate

Structure-borne noise. Vibration isolation, hanger selection and pipe restraint appear nowhere in an airborne calculation. Below about 60 Hz it becomes hard to say whether an occupant is hearing sound or feeling vibration. As a rough conversion for a vibrating surface treated as an infinite plane, sound pressure runs about 34 dB below the vibration level referenced to 10−9 m/s.

Flanking paths. A lightweight roof under a rooftop unit will carry radiated sound into the space below and bypass every attenuator in the duct. So will a continuous ceiling void shared between two rooms.

Diffuser and grille self-noise. On tight guest room criteria the terminal is often the limiting element rather than the fan. Get the rating for the terminal at its actual neck velocity and throw, and add it as a separate path.

Duct-generated and regenerated noise. Dampers near a diffuser, sharp transitions and high velocities all regenerate noise downstream of your attenuation. The tool accepts these figures but will not invent them.

Multi-fan tone phasing. Two fans running at slightly different speeds produce a beat at the difference frequency, which is often far more objectionable than either fan alone. A common drive across all condenser fans avoids it.

Before you put this in a submittal

The attenuation values built into this tool are indicative typical figures, not product data. They are here so you can size up a problem quickly and see which direction to push. For a submittal, replace every element with the manufacturer's tested insertion loss and state your source.

  • Check the external static the data was measured at. If your selection runs at higher ESP than the test point, the fan is working harder and the published spectrum understates it.
  • Calculate every path, not just the convenient one. A supply path that arrives 10 dB below the total is contributing nothing. If your calculation passes and the room does not, the path you left out is the reason.
  • Below 63 Hz, no attenuation is credited here. Published insertion loss data rarely extends that low, and crediting some would be the optimistic error rather than the safe one.
  • Watch the letter, not just the number. A spectrum that passes NC but rates rumbly will still generate complaints in a bedroom.
  • Criteria and octave bands are instruments, not the objective. The objective is that the majority of occupants do not find the sound objectionable, and that judgement is subjective. A room can meet its number and still be wrong.
